Glassbox Visual Identity & Editorial Design System
Version 1.0 — Art direction foundation

1. The creative direction
Counted clarity, told visually.

Glassbox turns large collections of job-market evidence into stories people can understand. Its visual identity should do the same: make complex information tangible, interesting and easy to investigate.

The visual experience should combine:

The editorial art direction and collage storytelling of Search Party.

The illustrated data storytelling and playful interactions of The Pudding.

The documentary pacing, visual explanations and motion of Johnny Harris.

The restraint, credibility and information hierarchy of The New York Times and Reuters.

These are references for qualities and techniques, not templates to reproduce.

The intended result: investigative journalism meets interactive data storytelling.

2. Editorial covers
Direction: Documentary editorial collage.

The cover should feel like the opening page of a well-researched investigation.

Visual ingredients:

Real documentary photography, preferably human, environmental or industry-specific.

Maps, diagrams, documents, charts and relevant source artifacts.

Layered compositions with deliberate cropping and overlapping.

Editorial annotations: lines, circles, grids, markings and labels.

Tactile backgrounds, subtle paper textures and restrained visual imperfections.

Strong editorial headline typography.

Composition rules:

One dominant visual subject.

Several supporting artifacts that add context.

A clear headline hierarchy.

A deliberate visual path through the composition.

Enough negative space to avoid making the cover feel like a scrapbook.

Every cover should have its own visual concept. We should not force every investigation into an identical collage template.

Artwork rule: No AI-generated artwork as final editorial cover art. Use human-created illustration, licensed or commissioned photography, original design and carefully constructed graphics.

3. Illustration and visual objects
Direction: Custom, characterful, editorial illustration.

Illustrations should explain something, not simply decorate empty space.

We should favour:

Hand-drawn or custom vector illustrations.

People representing professions and roles.

Objects, tools and environments associated with specific industries.

Small visual metaphors that make abstract concepts understandable.

Purposeful imperfections and human character.

Avoid:

Generic corporate illustrations.

Generic 3D illustrations.

Random decorative icons.

Stock illustrations that could belong to any SaaS product.

Existing mascots and icons
Existing Glassbox mascots, icons and visual assets are part of the identity. They should be inventoried and preserved before we create replacements.

New assets should extend the existing visual vocabulary rather than introduce an unrelated illustration style.

4. Data visualisation
Direction: Charts that tell stories.

A chart should communicate an argument, reveal a pattern or help someone explore evidence.

References:

The Pudding's ability to turn data into visual experiences.

Johnny Harris's use of maps, diagrams and visual explanations.

The clarity and discipline of established editorial data journalism.

Glassbox conventions to preserve:

Lime green represents skills present on a CV.

Grey hatching represents skills not yet present on a CV.

Small squares can represent individual jobs.

Every chart communicates its sample size and relevant date.

Labels and annotations explain what the reader is seeing.

New visual formats to explore:

Illustrated populations of people or objects.

Small multiples for comparing professions, skills or markets.

Maps and spatial visualisations.

Evidence cards and reconstructed job listings.

Flow diagrams showing how a process works.

Interactive charts that reveal detail through hover, tap or scroll.

Visual comparisons that let readers inspect two sides of an argument.

Important: Visual creativity must never compromise statistical accuracy. Every chart needs a clear denominator, timeframe, source and explanation where relevant.

5. Typography
The typography should establish a recognisable editorial voice.

Three roles:

Display typography

Expressive editorial serif.

Used for investigation headlines and major narrative moments.

Strong character without sacrificing readability.

Reading typography

Highly readable sans-serif.

Used for article body text, explanations and supporting information.

Data typography

Monospaced or technically styled type.

Used for counts, dates, source labels, chart annotations and technical details.

We should select actual typefaces after inspecting the existing website and brand assets. We should not introduce fonts that conflict with the current product identity.

6. Colour
The existing Glassbox palette remains the foundation.

Lime green: skills found on a CV.

Grey and hatching: skills not yet found on a CV.

Existing neutral colours: backgrounds, typography and supporting information.

Editorial investigations may use additional colours when the subject requires them. These should be introduced deliberately and consistently within an individual story.

A restrained red annotation accent is worth testing for editorial covers, but it should not become a new default brand colour without a broader brand review.

Colour must never be the only way information is distinguished.

7. Motion and interaction
Direction: Motion that reveals evidence.

Animation is part of the storytelling, not an afterthought.

Possible techniques:

Scroll-triggered chart reveals.

Animated counters showing how a number changes.

Highlighting evidence as the reader reaches a relevant paragraph.

Step-by-step diagrams.

Interactive comparisons.

Hover and tap states that reveal additional information.

Transitions between different states of a visualisation.

Map movements and visual journeys where geographically meaningful.

Motion principles:

Every animation must have a communication purpose.

The reader must remain in control.

Important information must remain available without animation.

Mobile interactions must be designed deliberately.

Respect reduced-motion preferences.

Avoid unnecessary movement and long transitions.

The existing AI-on-your-CV investigation is an important implementation reference. We should inspect its actual animation components before deciding what to reuse.

8. Evidence and source presentation
Evidence should be visible and understandable.

Use:

Reconstructed job cards.

Short, attributed source excerpts.

Highlighted requirements from job descriptions.

Document fragments and diagrams.

Methodology notes beside relevant visualisations.

Clear source references and dates.

Reconstructed artifacts must be labelled as reconstructions. We must not make an editorial graphic look like an original source document if it is not one.

9. Editorial page rhythm
An investigation should not be a long wall of text with a few charts inserted.

A possible rhythm:

Strong editorial cover.

Opening argument.

First evidence visualisation.

Explanation and interpretation.

Documentary or illustrated visual.

A more substantial interactive experience.

Further evidence and counterpoints.

Final synthesis.

Methodology and sources.

The visuals should appear where they advance the argument. Not every section needs an illustration, and not every chart needs animation.

10. Asset creation and production rules
Preferred production methods:

Human-created illustrations.

Licensed or commissioned photography.

Original SVG graphics.

Hand-built CSS and Canvas visualisations.

Custom chart components.

Purpose-built animations.

Carefully designed editorial collages.

AI can assist with implementation, code, layout exploration and technical production. It should not replace the human-created editorial artwork.

11. Recognition test
Every new visual should pass these questions:

Does it communicate something meaningful?

Does it feel like Glassbox?

Is it visually interesting without becoming distracting?

Is the evidence represented honestly?

Does it work on mobile?

Does it add something that ordinary text could not communicate as effectively?

The ultimate test:

Could someone recognise this as a Glassbox investigation without seeing the logo?

If not, our visual identity needs more work.
